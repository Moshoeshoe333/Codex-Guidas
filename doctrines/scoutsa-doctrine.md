# Grassroots Contextual Engineering

**Status:** Adopted design doctrine; project controls require separate verification.  
**Primary context:** ScoutSA and community-facing systems, with transfer only where relevant.

## 1. Design for lived reality

A product is not designed for an abstract “user.” It is designed for people with specific time, money, devices, connectivity, language, transport, safety and institutional constraints.

For ScoutSA, the initial context includes grassroots football in the Vaal Triangle / Sedibeng and the wider Gauteng ecosystem. These realities must be confirmed with community participants rather than assumed from a desk.

## 2. Safeguarding is a release precondition

When a system touches sport, youth or vulnerable communities, safeguarding is not a later enhancement. It is a prerequisite for any live use that could expose people to harm.

For ScoutSA this includes, at minimum:
- verified identity and role for adults who can access or act on opportunities;
- guardian consent and controlled participation for minors;
- no unsafe private adult-to-minor contact paths;
- no public exposure of minors' personal contact details or sensitive information;
- clear reporting, escalation, correction and removal routes;
- minimal data collection, purpose limitation and controlled retention;
- abuse-case testing and documented operational ownership.

A written policy alone does not prove these controls are implemented or effective. No public minor profiles, contact features or live opportunities until the relevant project gates, legal review, access controls, operational procedures and safeguarding tests are satisfied. This doctrine is not a legal compliance certification.

## 3. Synthetic evidence and empirical reality

Synthetic data can show that code handles known inputs and expected edge cases. It cannot establish that a real community wants the product, trusts it, can afford it, understands it or benefits from it.

Validation should progress deliberately:
1. synthetic tests and threat scenarios;
2. tabletop walkthroughs with relevant adult stakeholders;
3. supervised usability sessions with informed consent;
4. observed task completion and friction measurement;
5. repeat use, correction behaviour and measurable value;
6. only then, carefully governed live pilots where authorized.

Record who participated, what they were asked to do, what was observed, what was not tested and any sampling bias. Do not treat silence, compliments, sign-ups or social-media interest as proof of sustained demand.

## 4. Frictionless dignity

Reduce unnecessary cognitive, financial and social cost:
- keep data and bandwidth use proportionate;
- support low-end devices and unreliable connectivity where feasible;
- make critical flows understandable in appropriate local languages;
- avoid costly or complex onboarding where a simpler route is safe;
- explain why data is collected and how it is used;
- provide accessible correction and appeal routes;
- do not make payment a condition of being seen or considered for an opportunity.

“Frictionless” never means removing consent, safeguarding, verification or meaningful user choice.

## 5. Community accountability

Design with community members, not merely for them. Engage organizers, coaches, guardians, players and other relevant stakeholders with clear expectations. Compensate or acknowledge contributors fairly where appropriate. Never imply endorsement or partnership without evidence.

## 6. Decision rule

A feature is not ready for live use until the user problem, local constraints, harm scenarios, data flows, responsible operator, support route and evidence requirements are understood. If those are unknown, keep the feature in research or prototype status.
